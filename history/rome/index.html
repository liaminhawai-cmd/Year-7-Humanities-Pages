Pliny on Vesuvius

A Roman source, analysed at every level. Extension. This CAT does not assess source analysis.

Three historical thinking skills, not a formatting checklist. Ancient Rome does not assess source analysis. That is CAT 1 and CAT 3. Its CAT is a tourist guidebook, and this wall carries the thinking that has to happen inside it: chronology and cause and effect from the CAT rubric, plus continuity and change from Kew's own Developmental Rubric.

The CAT's other two rows are deliberately not here. Presentation is about laying a document out; metacognition is about managing your own learning. Both may be worth marking, but neither is a way of thinking about the past, and a wall modelling “present information creatively” would teach formatting rather than history.

Continuity and change earns its place because the genre demands it: a traveller arriving in 300 CE walks into a city already a thousand years old. Some of what they meet has never changed and some of it just has, saying which, and why, is the skill.

Draft. Written and levelled against both rubrics, but not yet taught.

The Holiday That Should Have Been Relaxing

Read across to see the recount grow from Level 6 to Level 9. Read down from a marked phrase to what it proves, then to the exact rubric wording underneath. The joke stays the same; the control of time and cause gets stronger.

The taskWrite a recount of a holiday. Make the reader feel as if they were there — even if “there” was a budget cabin, a rainstorm and one deeply unhelpful seagull.

Worked example On Monday morning, we arrived at the beach house. That afternoon, Dad tried to put up the tent while the rest of us ate chips and offered “help” from a safe distance. Because the wind was getting stronger, the tent flew away, so we chased it down the beach. The next day, we went kayaking, although I spent most of the trip going backwards. On Monday 8 July, we arrived at the beach house and unpacked. Later that afternoon, Dad put up the tent. The wind suddenly became stronger, which made the tent lift off the ground, and we had to chase it. The following morning, we went kayaking. Because I had not listened to the instructor, I paddled backwards and crashed into a bush. On Monday 8 July, during the first afternoon of our six-day holiday, we arrived at the beach house and unloaded the car. Over the next few hours, Dad assembled the tent while the wind gradually increased. As the gusts became stronger, one corner lifted, then the whole tent rose into the air. This meant that our relaxing first afternoon turned into a 400-metre sprint along the beach. By Tuesday morning, we had recovered enough to go kayaking. Since I had ignored the instructor's warning to keep my paddle low, I steered directly into a bush, which explained why everyone else was laughing. From Monday 8 July to Saturday 13 July, our supposedly peaceful six-day holiday became a sequence of increasingly ridiculous problems. On the first afternoon, while Dad was putting up the tent, the wind rose steadily. The stronger the gusts became, the more the tent pulled against its pegs until one corner finally escaped. Because nobody had packed anything heavy enough to hold it down, the entire tent became a very large, very expensive kite, and we spent the next ten minutes chasing it. The following morning, we went kayaking. Having ignored the instructor's warning about the current, I drifted into a bush; consequently, the instructor had to rescue me while my family laughed from the shore. For the remaining four days, we kept the tent indoors.
What it proves
■Uses simple time markers: Monday morning, that afternoon, the next day.
●Names a clear cause and result: the strong wind causes the tent to fly away.
■Adds a specific date and more controlled sequencing: 8 July, later that afternoon, the following morning.
●Explains the chain more clearly: wind increases → tent lifts → family chases it; poor listening → kayaking crash.
■Places events inside periods and gives their duration: the first afternoon of our six-day holiday, over the next few hours, by Tuesday morning.
●Shows developing causation rather than a single “because”: gusts → corner lifts → tent rises → relaxing afternoon becomes a sprint.
■Controls the whole time span: 8–13 July, first afternoon, following morning, remaining four days. The reader can see both sequence and duration.
●Builds a causal chain with precise links: wind → escaping peg → no weight → flying tent, then ignored warning → current → bush → rescue.

Teacher note: The joke is deliberately ordinary: the same holiday remains the same event at every level. What changes is the student's control of time and causal relationships. The Level 8 model is the “strong model” column, but Level 9 is not simply “more adjectives” — it makes the causal structure and duration more precise.

Water in the World · 2.3 The Significance of Water

Worked example: writing a TEEL paragraph

Content for Self-Assessment: Interconnection

Question: Explain the significance of the Martuwarra Fitzroy River to its Traditional Owners.

Click each part of TEEL to see what it is doing.

Level:

TWater is important to Aboriginal people.

EPeople say the river is really important to them and it should not be wrecked.

EThis shows water is important.

LSo it is important.

TWater is important to the Traditional Owners of the Martuwarra Fitzroy River.

EDr Anne Poelina says the river is a living ancestor. The government wants to take water out of it for farming.

EThis shows the people and the river are connected.

LSo water is important to them.

TThe Martuwarra Fitzroy River is significant to its Traditional Owners because they see it as a living ancestor rather than a resource.

EDr Anne Poelina, a Nyikina Warrwa woman, describes the river as “an ancestral serpent being with a right to not only live but a right to live and flow”. Yi Martuwarra Traditional Owner Anthony McLarty says “It is a living system. It has the right to life.”

EThis shows the connection is a relationship, not just a use. The river provides food and identity, and in return the people have a responsibility to protect it.

LWater at the Martuwarra is therefore valued spiritually and culturally, not only economically.

TThe Martuwarra Fitzroy River is significant to its Traditional Owners because they see it as a living ancestor, but who controls the river has changed.

EDr Anne Poelina, a Nyikina Warrwa woman, describes the river as “an ancestral serpent being with a right to not only live but a right to live and flow”. Hancock Agriculture has sought a licence to pump 325 gigalitres a year, and in 2019 the state government was considering allocations of up to 600 gigalitres.

EThese are two different values of water in conflict. The conflict exists because colonisation changed who makes the decisions: Traditional Owners cared for the river for thousands of years, but water licences are now granted by the state government. This is why six Traditional Owner groups signed the Fitzroy River declaration in 2016 and formed the Martuwarra Fitzroy River Council.

LThe interconnection between the people and the river has therefore changed from custodianship to a contest over control.

TThe Martuwarra Fitzroy River shows how the interconnection between people and water has changed over time, and why that change is now being contested.

EDr Anne Poelina, a Nyikina Warrwa woman, describes the river as “an ancestral serpent being with a right to not only live but a right to live and flow”, while Hancock Agriculture has sought a licence to pump 325 gigalitres a year. At Menindee on the Darling River, up to a million native fish died in 2019, blamed on drought and over-extraction, the comparison Traditional Owners themselves use.

EFor thousands of years the interconnection was custodianship: people cared for the river and the river sustained them. Colonisation moved the decisions to the state, and the pressure now is commercial agriculture. The Murray–Darling shows what happens when that change runs unchecked, extraction outgrew the river and the interconnection collapsed. The Fitzroy River declaration (2016) and the Martuwarra Fitzroy River Council are an attempt by Traditional Owners to reverse the change before it reaches that point.

LAnalysed over time, the significance of the Martuwarra is inseparable from who controls it, and the Murray–Darling comparison shows the next change is a choice, not an inevitability.

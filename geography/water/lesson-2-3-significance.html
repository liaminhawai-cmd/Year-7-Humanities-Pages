Water in the World · Lesson 2.3 · teacher plan

The Significance of Water

Rebuilt around one question, three contrasting case studies, and the school's Interconnection ladder. Content: VC2HG8K02 (environmental, economic, cultural, spiritual & aesthetic values of water, including for First Nations Peoples).

The spine of the lesson

Everyone values water. So who gets to decide what a river is for?

One question that carries the values content, the case studies and the assessment. It's answerable at every level: a Year 5–6 student can identify who's connected to a river; a Year 9–10 student can explain how law, history and power decide whose values count.

Success criteria — from the continuum, not invented

The old criteria (“I can identify the uses of water in Aboriginal communities”) were recall, so students self-assessed against something the lesson never taught. These are the 1.2 Interconnection rungs, verbatim, with what they look like in this lesson.

Level 6“I can identify how places, people, and environments are connected.”
→ I can name who is connected to the Martuwarra and how.
Level 7“I can identify interconnections within and between places, people, and environments.”
→ I can identify that different groups value the same river differently.
Level 8“I can describe interconnections within and between places, people, and environments.”
→ I can describe those values with evidence from the case study.
Level 9“I can explain interconnections … over time and at different scales.”
→ I can explain how colonisation and water law shaped who decides today.
Level 10“I can analyse interconnections … over time and at different scales.”
→ I can analyse why outcomes differed across the three communities.

What I changed, and why

The values table was the weak point. Filling in “economic / cultural / spiritual” with a definition and an example is recall — and it teaches something false: that these are four separate boxes. For First Nations Peoples they are one relationship. Fixed below as values in tension.
The jigsaw had no accountability. “Read a section, report back” lets one student carry the group. Fixed with three different case studies, a role each, and a comparison only completable if all three report honestly.
The TEEL was bolted on. No model, no criteria. Fixed with levelled model paragraphs so students can see the difference between describing and explaining.

Cultural protocols — worth reading before you teach it

  • Warning to give the class: the Australians Together resource and some videos contain images, voices or names of people who have died.
  • Use the Custodians' own voices. The videos are Elders speaking for themselves — that's the source, not a summary of them.
  • Don't ask students to write “as” a First Nations person, imagine being one, or invent a Dreaming story. Dreaming stories belong to specific groups and are told by those with the right to tell them. Students analyse and respond; they don't roleplay.
  • Name the group, not “Aboriginal people” in general — Walmajarri, Bunuba, Gunditjmara, Yolŋu. Precision is respect, and it's also better geography.
  • Use present tense. Connection to water is ongoing and current, not historical.
  • Beware false balance in the final discussion. “Both sides have a point” misreads the situation: First Nations Peoples hold well under 1% of water entitlements despite native title over roughly 40% of Australia's land. The honest question is about power and process, not a tie to be split.
  • Local first, if you can. Wurundjeri Woi-wurrung Country and the Birrarung (Yarra) are on the school's doorstep. The Yarra River Protection (Wilip-gin Birrarung murron) Act 2017 — “keep the Birrarung alive” in Woi-wurrung, and the first law in Australia co-titled in a Traditional Owner language — declares the river “one living and integrated natural entity” and created the Birrarung Council to speak for it. That's this lesson's argument, legislated, 30 minutes from the classroom.

The lesson

One period (~60 min), or run Phases 3–5 over two if the jigsaw is doing real work.

1 · Hook — one river, four claims8 min

Put the Martuwarra Fitzroy River on the board with four short claims about what it's for:

  • a living ancestor and a law-carrier — Senior Custodians of several language groups
  • an irrigation source — the WA Government's plan to take 300+ billion litres a year for farming and industry
  • a tourism asset — Danggu (Geikie Gorge), on Bunuba Country, one of the Kimberley's most-visited sites
  • a habitat — the world's last stronghold of the critically endangered freshwater sawfish

Ask: “All four are true. Only one group's view will decide what happens. Whose — and why?” Take 60 seconds of turn-and-talk. Don't resolve it; that's the lesson.

2 · Values in tension (replaces the values table)12 min

Same five value types the curriculum names — but applied to one water body, by whom, and then tested for conflict.

The insight to land — say it explicitly. Ask: “Which rows were hard to keep separate?” For the Traditional Custodians, spiritual, cultural, environmental and economic value are one relationship with Country, not four boxes. The table is a Western tool; noticing where it breaks down is the geographic insight. Students who write “these overlap and can't really be separated” are thinking at Level 8–9, not failing the task.
3 · Jigsaw — three communities, three outcomes20 min

Groups of three. Each student takes one case — they're different cases, not sections of one article, so nobody can free-ride and the comparison is impossible without all three. Expert phase (8 min, read/watch alone or with same-case students) → home phase (12 min, report back and fill the comparison).

the fight, unresolved

A · Martuwarra Fitzroy River

700 km through the Kimberley; Australia's largest Aboriginal heritage site. WA Government proposes taking 300+ billion litres for farming and industry. Custodians from several language groups formed the Martuwarra Fitzroy River Council; 27,000+ people petitioned to protect it in May 2023.

Sources: Voices of the River — “Joe: I sing this song, Martuwarra” (1:30); Guardian, “Fitzroy River: the push to prevent a repeat of the Murray–Darling Basin disaster”; handout pp. 9–11.

rights won

B · Budj Bim, Gunditjmara Country

Gunditjmara People built a vast eel-trapping aquaculture system and stone villages over thousands of years. Colonisers diverted and damaged it. Native title won in 2008; UNESCO World Heritage listed 2019; water reallocated by Southern Rural Water, so the aquaculture and kooyong (eel) practices are being re-established.

Sources: NMA, “What Makes Lake Condah a Defining Moment?” (2:34); NMA “Budj Bim Cultural Landscape”; Gunditj Mirring (GMTOAC) website; handout pp. 18–19.

an economy from water

C · Mandjawuy, Yolŋu Country

A small community in north-east Arnhem Land runs Knowledge Water, a spring water business, to become more self-sufficient — creating jobs and funding a teacher so Yolŋu children can be educated on Country instead of leaving.

Sources: Knowledge Water Story (3:04); Knowledge Water “Our business” / “Our story”; ABC, “Aboriginal community launching bottled water business…”; handout pp. 22–23.

The question that needs all three: “All three communities value water deeply. Why did the three end up with such different amounts of control over it?” That's the Level 9–10 move — comparison across places, over time.

4 · The mechanism — why control differs8 min

Short teacher input, because students can't explain the pattern without it. A timeline on the board:

WhenWhat happened to who decides
pre-1788First Nations law and lore: everyone responsible for maintaining and caring for water.
1788→Colonial law ties water rights to land ownership; terra nullius denies First Nations ownership — so it denies water.
1918–1970Engineering era — dams, locks, weirs. River flows and river health decline.
1992–93Mabo overturns terra nullius; Native Title Act. Water access recognised, but limited to domestic and cultural use.
2007Echuca Declaration — Traditional Custodians define cultural flows: water held by and for First Nations Peoples, like environmental flows but for cultural life.
nowNative title covers ~40% of Australia's land; First Nations Peoples hold under 1% of water entitlements. Victoria's Water is Life roadmap (2022) and the return of 2 billion litres to Gunaikurnai People are small steps.

Land it: “Values didn't decide this. Law did — and law can change. That's why Budj Bim looks different from the Martuwarra.”

5 · Write — TEEL, with the level made visible12 min

Task: “Choose one case study. Explain how water connects people, place and environment — and who decides what happens to it.” Show the three models first (2 min), then write. Students mark which model theirs is closest to and what they'd add to move up one rung.

Level 6–7 identify
Water is important to First Nations Peoples for spiritual reasons. The Martuwarra Fitzroy River is important to the Traditional Custodians. Elders say the river is like a living ancestor. The government wants to take water for farming. This shows people are connected to the river.
Why it stops here: names a connection, but no evidence and no explanation of how or why. To move up: add a figure or a quote, and say what the connection means.
Level 8 describe, with evidence

TFor the Traditional Custodians of the Martuwarra Fitzroy River, water holds spiritual, cultural and economic value at the same time.

EThe river runs 700 km through the Kimberley and is Australia's largest Aboriginal heritage site. Walmajarri Elder Joe Brown sings the river, describing it as a living ancestor, while the WA Government has proposed extracting more than 300 billion litres a year for farming and industry.

EThis means the river is not only a water supply. It carries law, story and identity, so damage to the river is also damage to the people connected to it.

LWater therefore connects people, culture and environment in a way the word “resource” cannot describe.

Why it's Level 8: describes the interconnection and quantifies it. To move up: explain why the disagreement exists — bring in time and scale.
Level 9–10 explain / analyse

TThe conflict over the Martuwarra Fitzroy River is not simply a clash of values — it is the result of who has held decision-making power over water since colonisation.

ECustodians from several language groups formed the Martuwarra Fitzroy River Council to defend the river as a living ancestor, and 27,000 people petitioned against the plan to extract 300+ billion litres.

EBecause colonial law tied water rights to land ownership, and terra nullius denied First Nations ownership until Mabo in 1992, First Nations Peoples today hold under 1% of Australia's water entitlements despite native title over about 40% of its land. The dispute therefore operates at several scales at once — a local river, a state government's economic plan, and a national legal history — and across more than two centuries.

LComparing Budj Bim, where native title and returned water allowed Gunditjmara aquaculture to be re-established, shows the difference is not how strongly each community values water, but whether the law recognises their authority over it.

Why it's Level 9–10: explains over time and at different scales, then analyses by comparing two cases.

Exit ticket (30 seconds, hands it to you as formative data)

“Cultural flows means water that is ______________ . One thing that would need to change for the Martuwarra to be protected like Budj Bim is ______________ .”

Two gaps worth knowing about

1. The Asia-region half of the descriptor

VC2HG8K02 asks for the values of water “including for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander Peoples and peoples of the Asia region.” The current 2.3 covers the first half only. Cheapest fix: a 5-minute contrast at the end of Phase 3 — the Mekong (flood season as a giver of silt and fish; a calendar, not a disaster) or the Ganges (a river that is legally and spiritually a living being). It also strengthens the lesson: two very different cultures both treat a river as a relative rather than a resource, which makes the point cross-cultural instead of exceptional.

2. Where this lands in the unit

2.3 is the values lesson, and it sets up 3.1–3.2 (hazards) and the Every Drop sim, where the same question returns as a decision: consult the custodian first, or don't. If you run the sim after this lesson, students meet Aunty Marion already knowing what a cultural flow is — and the FPIC mechanic reads as a rights issue rather than a game rule.

Assessment trail: TEEL paragraph (formative, marked against 1.2) + exit ticket + the students' own comparison table. Enough to place every student on the Interconnection ladder without a separate test.
